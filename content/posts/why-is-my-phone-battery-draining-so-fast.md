<h2>Read the battery health number before anything else</h2>
<p>Battery health, sometimes called <strong>maximum capacity</strong>, tells you how much charge the battery still holds compared with when it was new. A lithium-ion phone battery loses capacity with every charge cycle, so a three-year-old phone holding 84% is normal wear, not a fault.</p>
<p>On an iPhone, open <strong>Settings &gt; Battery &gt; Battery Health &amp; Charging</strong> and read Maximum Capacity. Apple&#x27;s own service criterion is <strong>80%</strong>: at or below that, the battery is officially due for replacement. Anything above about 85% usually means wear is not your main problem.</p>
<p>Android is messier. Samsung hides the number and puts a battery diagnostic in the Samsung Members app. Pixel shows charging optimisation but not a capacity percentage on many versions, so check <strong>Settings &gt; Battery</strong>, then your manufacturer&#x27;s diagnostics app. Where there is no reading at all, skip to the usage graph section; that path works on every phone.</p>
<p>Do this first because it splits the case in two. A genuinely worn battery drains fast at all times, on every app, even idle overnight. Everything else on this page explains the other kind of drain: sudden, patchy, or tied to one app or one situation.</p>

<h2>Use the battery usage graph to find the app eating the charge</h2>
<p>Both operating systems keep a per-app record of what used the battery. On iPhone it is <strong>Settings &gt; Battery</strong> and scroll to the list under the graph; on Android it is <strong>Settings &gt; Battery &gt; Battery usage</strong> (the exact path varies by brand and version). The graph at the top shows battery level against time, and the bars underneath show <strong>screen-on time</strong> versus background time.</p>
<p>Read it with two questions. Is one app wildly out of proportion, say 30% of use when you barely opened it? Then that app is your suspect. Does battery level fall steeply overnight or while the phone sits idle with the screen off? Then background activity, not your thumbs, is doing the draining.</p>
<p>The graph goes back 24 hours or 10 days depending on the toggle. Use the 10-day view to separate a one-off heavy day from a steady slide. A steady slide with no single culprit points back to the worn-battery diagnosis from the previous section.</p>
<p>Fix what you find: force-stop or uninstall a rogue app, restrict its background activity (long-press the app icon &gt; App info &gt; Battery on Android; iPhone offers background app refresh under <strong>Settings &gt; General &gt; Background App Refresh</strong>). Then watch one full charge cycle before judging the result.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Check this:</b> Idle drain is the tell: losing more than a few percent overnight with the screen off means background activity, not an old battery.</div>
<h2>Sudden drain right after an update or a new app is software</h2>
<p>Timing is the strongest clue you have. A battery that wears out fades over months. If your phone went from a full day of use to half a day <strong>within a day or two of an update or a new install</strong>, the battery did not suddenly wear out; something changed in software.</p>
<p>After a major OS update, drain for a day or two is expected. The phone re-indexes photos, re-downloads content, re-syncs accounts and spends time learning your usage patterns for charge management. Watch the usage graph during this window and you will see system processes near the top. It settles on its own.</p>
<p>A new app is easier to judge. If drain started the same day you installed something, uninstall it and see whether the next charge cycle returns to normal. A rogue app stuck in a crash loop or polling a service in the background can chew through a battery even when you never open it.</p>
<p>If an OS update is the trigger and drain persists past a few days, try a restart first, then check the app list for anything running hot. As a last resort a backup and reset clears corrupted settings, but that is a couple of hours of work, so do the cheap checks first.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Common mistake:</b> Replacing a battery the same week as an OS update wastes the money; give the phone two or three days to settle first.</div>
<h2>Weak cell signal drains the battery even in your pocket</h2>
<p>The radio is one of the biggest hidden consumers. When signal is weak, the phone <strong>raises transmit power and keeps re-searching</strong> for a better tower, and it does this continuously whether you are using the phone or not. One or two bars in a car park, rural area, basement or office core can cut standby time dramatically, and no battery replacement will fix it.</p>
<p>The usage graph usually shows Screen mostly, so people miss this cause. A better tell: the phone gets warm in your pocket while idle, and drain is bad in one location and fine elsewhere. That location-dependence is the signature of a signal problem.</p>
<p>What to do depends on where you are. In a genuinely dead zone, turn on <strong>airplane mode</strong> and let the phone sleep. In places with poor cellular but decent Wi-Fi, enable <strong>Wi-Fi calling</strong> (Settings &gt; Phone &gt; Wi-Fi Calling on iPhone; it sits under network or calling settings on Android) so the cellular radio can rest. Near home, a signal-boosting Wi-Fi network plus Wi-Fi calling beats standing by the window.</p>
<p>Bluetooth and always-on location rarely cause this kind of drain by themselves, but check them under Settings if the graph shows system services high on the list. Location set to &#x27;always&#x27; for many apps is a common quiet consumer.</p>

<h2>A new phone draining fast in the first days is usually normal</h2>
<p>A freshly set up phone works hard for its first few days. It restores apps and data, re-syncs email and cloud accounts, indexes photos for search, and learns your charging pattern for optimised charging. All of that runs in the background on top of whatever you are actually doing with it.</p>
<p>Expect two or three days of heavier drain, sometimes up to a week on slower networks and bigger restores. Unless the phone is getting uncomfortably hot while idle, do nothing. The drain drops on its own once indexing and syncing finish.</p>
<p>The one exception is a defective unit. If the phone still feels hot in your pocket after a week, or loses a third of its charge overnight with the screen off, check the usage graph for a stuck process and contact the manufacturer&#x27;s support while you are inside the warranty or return window. That window is exactly why you should not wait a month before judging a new phone.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Check this:</b> New phone still hot and draining after a week? That is a warranty case, not a settings case. Act inside the return window.</div>
<h2>Settings to change today, and charging habits you can ignore</h2>
<p>These settings cut drain on any phone, worn battery or not, and none of them cost you anything you will miss. Work down the list once and you are done; none of this is a substitute for the diagnosis above.</p>
<p><strong>Brightness</strong>: keep auto-brightness on; the screen is the single largest consumer on most phones. <strong>Refresh rate</strong>: on phones with 120 Hz screens, Settings &gt; Display lets you choose standard 60 Hz for long battery life. <strong>Background activity</strong>: trim Background App Refresh on iPhone, or Battery restrictions on Android, for the apps you never need instantly. <strong>Always-on display</strong>: turn it off if your model has one; it costs a few percent a day. <strong>Location</strong>: set apps to &#x27;while using&#x27; instead of &#x27;always&#x27;.</p>
<p>Then the myth. Closing apps you are not using does not save battery on modern phones; suspended apps cost almost nothing and reopening them costs more, so you can ignore that advice entirely. Wireless charging, fast charging and overnight charging do not explain sudden drain either. The one charging habit with real long-term value is avoiding leaving the phone at 100% in heat, such as a dashboard in summer, because heat ages lithium batteries. Everything else people tell you about charging is a red herring for a battery draining faster this week.</p>
<ul><li>Brightness: leave auto-brightness on</li><li>Refresh rate: 60 Hz when you need the runtime</li><li>Background App Refresh: trim, don&#x27;t disable globally</li><li>Always-on display: off if you want the extra percent</li><li>Location: &#x27;while using&#x27; for everything that doesn&#x27;t need &#x27;always&#x27;</li></ul>
<div class="note warn"><b>Common mistake:</b> Force-closing apps to save battery is a myth; suspended apps cost almost nothing and reopening them uses more.</div>
<div class="note"><b>Our take:</b> Diagnose in this order: health number, usage graph, then signal and recent changes. Charging habits come last.</div>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>How do I check battery health on Android if there is no percentage shown?</h3>
<p>Samsung hides it in the Samsung Members app under diagnostics, and some brands put a battery status in Settings &gt; Battery. If your phone shows nothing, dial codes and third-party apps are unreliable; use the usage graph and idle-drain test instead, or ask a repair shop to read the capacity.</p>
<h3>My battery dropped to 84% capacity in a year. Is that normal?</h3>
<p>It is on the faster side but not alarming, especially with daily fast charging, heat, or heavy use. Apple and most makers consider 80% the end of service life, so you have room left. Keep it out of heat and avoid pinning it at 100% for hours to slow further loss.</p>
<h3>Can a rogue app damage the battery permanently?</h3>
<p>Not quickly. Sustained heat is what ages a lithium battery, so an app that keeps the phone hot for weeks does accelerate wear. Uninstall it and the battery keeps whatever capacity remains; no app creates permanent drain by itself.</p>
<h3>Does 5G drain the battery faster than 4G?</h3>
<p>It can in weak 5G areas, where the phone keeps switching between 5G and 4G. If your area has patchy 5G coverage, setting the phone to 4G/LTE under Settings &gt; Mobile Data (path varies by model) often improves standby noticeably.</p>
<h3>Is it worth replacing the battery at 85% capacity?</h3>
<p>Usually not yet. At 85% you should get most of a normal day. The economics improve when you drop under 80% or the phone shuts down early on cold days despite showing charge remaining.</p>
