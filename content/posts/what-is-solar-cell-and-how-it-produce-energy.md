<div class="key"><b>In one sentence:</b> a solar cell is a thin slice of semiconductor (usually silicon) built so that when light knocks electrons loose, a built-in electric field pushes them in one direction, creating a current you can use. Many cells wired together make a solar panel.</div>

<h2>How it works, step by step</h2>
<ol class="steps">
<li><b>Two layers of silicon.</b> Pure silicon is "doped" with tiny amounts of other elements. One layer gets extra electrons (n-type, often using phosphorus); the other gets "holes", places missing an electron (p-type, often using boron).</li>
<li><b>The junction.</b> Where the layers meet, electrons and holes drift across and cancel out, leaving a thin zone with a built-in electric field. This is the <b>p-n junction</b>, the same structure found in diodes.</li>
<li><b>Light frees electrons.</b> Sunlight arrives as packets of energy called photons. A photon with enough energy knocks an electron free, leaving a hole behind. This is the <b>photovoltaic effect</b>.</li>
<li><b>The field sorts them.</b> The junction's field sweeps freed electrons to the n side and holes to the p side, so they cannot simply recombine.</li>
<li><b>Current flows.</b> Metal contacts on the front (the thin silver lines you see) and back collect the charges. Connect a load and electrons flow through it: electricity.</li>
</ol>
<p>A single silicon cell produces only about 0.5–0.6 volts, so panels wire dozens of cells in series to reach useful voltages. The output is direct current (DC); an inverter converts it to the alternating current (AC) used in homes.</p>

<h2>Why panels are not 100% efficient</h2>
<p>Some light is reflected, some photons have too little energy to free an electron, and photons with extra energy waste the excess as heat. For a single-junction silicon cell, physics sets a theoretical ceiling of roughly 33% (the Shockley–Queisser limit). Good commercial panels today convert a little over 20% of sunlight into electricity.</p>
<p>Heat also lowers output: panels produce slightly less on a hot day than on a cold, bright one.</p>

<h2>Types of solar cells</h2>
<table><thead><tr><th>Type</th><th>What it is</th><th>Where you see it</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Monocrystalline silicon</td><td>Cut from a single crystal; uniform black look; highest common efficiency</td><td>Most new rooftop panels</td></tr>
<tr><td>Polycrystalline silicon</td><td>Many crystals melted together; blue speckled look; cheaper, slightly less efficient</td><td>Older and budget installations</td></tr>
<tr><td>Thin film (CdTe, CIGS, amorphous Si)</td><td>Very thin layers on glass or flexible sheets</td><td>Utility-scale farms, calculators, flexible panels</td></tr>
<tr><td>Perovskite and tandem cells</td><td>New crystal materials, often stacked on silicon to capture more of the spectrum</td><td>Labs and early commercial products</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Tandem cells that stack perovskite on top of silicon have beaten the single-junction silicon limit in laboratories, which is why they are one of the most watched areas in energy research. Long-term durability is the main challenge before they become common on roofs.</p>

<h2>What this means for you</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>Portable solar</b> panels can top up phones and power stations on camping trips, but output drops sharply in shade or cloud.</li>
<li><b>Rooftop solar</b> value depends on your sunshine, electricity prices and local incentives.</li>
<li><b>Solar gadgets</b> (garden lights, chargers) use small cells, so expect modest power.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Do solar panels work on cloudy days?</h3>
<p>Yes, but output falls a lot, often to a fraction of full-sun output depending on how thick the cloud is.</p>
<h3>Do solar cells work with indoor light?</h3>
<p>Slightly. Indoor light is far weaker than sunlight, which is why calculator cells are tiny and need very little power.</p>
<h3>How long do solar panels last?</h3>
<p>Quality panels typically carry 25-year performance warranties and lose a small percentage of output each year.</p>
