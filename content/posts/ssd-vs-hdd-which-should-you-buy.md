<div class="key"><b>Simple advice:</b> your computer's main drive should be an <b>SSD</b>, ideally NVMe. Use a <b>hard drive (HDD)</b> only where you need lots of cheap space and speed matters less: backups, media libraries and NAS storage.</div>

<h2>The three types</h2>
<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>NVMe SSD</th><th>SATA SSD</th><th>Hard drive (HDD)</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>How it stores data</td><td>Flash memory chips over PCIe</td><td>Flash memory over the older SATA link</td><td>Spinning magnetic platters and a moving head</td></tr>
<tr><td>Typical sequential speed</td><td>About 3,000–7,000+ MB/s (PCIe 3/4/5)</td><td>Up to about 550 MB/s</td><td>About 100–250 MB/s</td></tr>
<tr><td>Feels like</td><td>Instant</td><td>Very fast</td><td>Slow to boot and load</td></tr>
<tr><td>Shape</td><td>M.2 stick (gum-sized)</td><td>2.5-inch box, or M.2 SATA</td><td>3.5-inch (desktop) or 2.5-inch (laptop)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Price per TB</td><td>Medium</td><td>Medium</td><td>Lowest</td></tr>
<tr><td>Noise and shock</td><td>Silent, shock resistant</td><td>Silent, shock resistant</td><td>Audible, sensitive to drops</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>The difference you notice most is not peak speed but <b>random access</b>: opening apps, booting and searching thousands of small files. SSDs do this vastly faster than hard drives because nothing has to physically move.</p>

<h2>Which to buy for…</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>A laptop or desktop system drive:</b> NVMe SSD, 1 TB if you can.</li>
<li><b>Upgrading an old laptop with a hard drive:</b> a 2.5-inch SATA SSD is the single best upgrade; check whether it has an M.2 slot first.</li>
<li><b>Gaming:</b> NVMe SSD. Modern games load much faster, and some console and PC games are designed around SSD speed.</li>
<li><b>Backups and photo archives:</b> external hard drive, for the lowest cost per terabyte.</li>
<li><b>NAS / home server:</b> NAS-rated hard drives for capacity, maybe an SSD cache.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Lifespan and reliability</h2>
<p>SSDs wear slowly with writes, measured as <b>TBW</b> (terabytes written). A typical 1 TB consumer SSD is rated for hundreds of TBW, far more than most people write in its useful life. Hard drives fail mechanically, often with warning signs like clicking. Either can fail without warning, which is why backups matter: see our <a href="/best-free-online-data-backup-service/">backup guide</a>.</p>
<p>One difference matters for recovery: deleted files on SSDs are usually gone quickly because of TRIM. More in <a href="/download-active-file-recovery-software-free/">how to recover deleted files</a>.</p>

<h2>Check before you buy</h2>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Does your computer have an <b>M.2 slot</b>, and does it support NVMe or only SATA? Check the manual or the maker's support page.</li>
<li>Which <b>PCIe generation</b>? A PCIe 4.0 drive works in a PCIe 3.0 slot, just at 3.0 speeds.</li>
<li>Plan how to <b>move your system</b>: many SSD makers provide free cloning software.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Is a PCIe 5.0 SSD worth it?</h3>
<p>For most people, no. In everyday use and gaming, the difference from a good PCIe 4.0 drive is small, while price and heat are higher.</p>
<h3>Do SSDs lose data if unplugged?</h3>
<p>Consumer SSDs can slowly lose data if left unpowered for very long periods, especially when warm. For long-term cold storage, hard drives and multiple backups are safer.</p>
