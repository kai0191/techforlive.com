<div class="key"><b>The balanced view:</b> phones are powerful tools, and most of their downsides come from <em>how</em> and <em>when</em> we use them: late at night, while driving, or in ways that crowd out sleep and face-to-face time. On radiation, a large 2024 review commissioned by the World Health Organization found no link between mobile phone use and brain cancer.</div>

<h2>Sleep</h2>
<p>Using a phone in bed delays sleep mostly because it keeps the brain engaged: one more video, one more message. Blue light from screens gets a lot of attention, but research suggests the stimulation of content matters at least as much as the light itself. Night modes help a little; putting the phone out of reach helps more.</p>

<h2>Attention and focus</h2>
<p>Constant notifications fragment attention, and switching tasks has a cost: it takes time to refocus on demanding work after an interruption. Some studies even suggest a phone simply sitting on the desk can be distracting, though findings vary. Batching notifications and using focus modes are simple fixes.</p>

<h2>Posture and eyes</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>"Text neck":</b> looking down for long periods strains the neck and shoulders. Raise the phone closer to eye level and take breaks.</li>
<li><b>Eye strain:</b> we blink less when staring at screens. The 20-20-20 habit helps: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 m) away for 20 seconds.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Driving and walking</h2>
<p>Using a phone while driving is one of the clearest dangers: it takes eyes, hands and attention off the road, and it is illegal to hold a phone while driving in many countries. Use Driving Focus or Driving Mode to silence notifications automatically.</p>

<h2>Mental health and relationships</h2>
<p>Research on smartphones, social media and wellbeing is active and sometimes contradictory. Heavy use is associated with poorer sleep and mood in some groups, especially teenagers, but phones also keep people connected and supported. The quality of use, such as passive scrolling versus talking to friends, seems to matter as much as the hours.</p>

<h2>What about phone radiation?</h2>
<p>Phones emit radiofrequency (RF) energy, which is non-ionising: it does not have enough energy to break chemical bonds or damage DNA directly the way X-rays can. In 2011 the WHO's cancer agency (IARC) classified RF fields as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B), a category meaning evidence is limited. Since then, a WHO-commissioned systematic review published in 2024, covering decades of studies, found no association between mobile phone use and brain cancers. Phones must also meet exposure limits (SAR) before sale. If you want to reduce exposure anyway, use speakerphone or earphones for long calls.</p>

<h2>Practical ways to use your phone better</h2>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Check your screen time: <b>Digital Wellbeing</b> on Android, <b>Screen Time</b> on iPhone.</li>
<li>Set app timers for the apps you lose time in.</li>
<li>Turn off non-essential notifications, and schedule a daily summary.</li>
<li>Use Bedtime / Sleep focus and charge the phone outside the bedroom.</li>
<li>Keep the phone face down or in another room during focused work and meals.</li>
</ol>

<h2>For parents</h2>
<p>Built-in family tools (Google Family Link, Apple Screen Time) set limits, approve apps and set downtime for children's phones. For shared family computers, dedicated monitoring software can add limits and activity reports.</p>

<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Is it bad to sleep with my phone next to the bed?</h3>
<p>The bigger issue is the temptation to use it, which delays sleep. Radiation from a phone on a nightstand is very low. Charging it across the room solves both concerns.</p>
<h3>How much screen time is too much?</h3>
<p>There is no single safe number for adults. Notice whether phone use is cutting into sleep, work, exercise or time with people; that is the real signal.</p>
