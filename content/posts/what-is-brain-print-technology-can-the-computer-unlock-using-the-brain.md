<div class="key"><b>In short:</b> a "brainprint" is a pattern in your brain's electrical activity, measured with EEG sensors, that is distinctive enough to tell you apart from other people. Researchers have shown it can identify small groups of volunteers in the lab with very high accuracy, but it is not yet a practical way to unlock everyday devices. For now, passkeys and fingerprints are what you should use.</div>

<h2>How brainprints work</h2>
<p>Neurons communicate with tiny electrical signals. Sensors on the scalp (electroencephalography, or EEG) pick up the combined activity of millions of them. When you see a picture or word, your brain produces a characteristic response called an event-related potential. Because brains differ in structure and in what they have learned, these responses vary from person to person.</p>
<p>A brainprint system shows you a sequence of stimuli, such as images of food, celebrities or words, records your responses, and compares them with a stored template, much like a fingerprint reader compares ridges.</p>

<h2>What research has shown</h2>
<p>A widely reported study from Binghamton University, published in 2016, presented 50 participants with hundreds of images while recording EEG. The researchers' system identified every participant correctly in their test. Other groups have explored "passthoughts", where users think of a specific song or action to authenticate.</p>
<p>These were controlled lab studies with relatively few people, wet or research-grade sensors and careful conditions. Accuracy in noisy real-world use, over months as brains and moods change, is still being studied.</p>

<h2>Why researchers are interested</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>Hard to steal:</b> you leave fingerprints on glasses, and faces are photographed everywhere, but brain responses are not left behind.</li>
<li><b>Cancellable:</b> unlike a fingerprint, the stimulus set can be changed if a template leaks, producing a new brainprint.</li>
<li><b>Liveness:</b> brain responses require a living, attentive person, which may help detect coercion or impersonation.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why it is not in your phone yet</h2>
<ul>
<li>Reliable EEG needs sensors in good contact with the scalp; headbands and earbuds with EEG exist but are less precise.</li>
<li>Authentication takes longer than a glance at a face sensor.</li>
<li>Brain signals change with tiredness, caffeine, medication and attention.</li>
<li><b>Privacy:</b> brain data can reveal more than identity, such as attention or emotional responses. Several jurisdictions have begun treating "neural data" as a specially protected category.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Can someone read my thoughts with a brainprint scanner?</h3>
<p>No. Authentication systems match patterns of response to known stimuli; they do not decode free thoughts. But brain data is sensitive, which is why privacy rules matter.</p>
<h3>Is this the same as Neuralink?</h3>
<p>No. Neuralink and similar brain-computer interfaces use implanted electrodes, mainly to help people with paralysis control devices. Brainprint research uses external EEG for identification.</p>
