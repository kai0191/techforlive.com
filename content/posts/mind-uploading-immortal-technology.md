<div class="key"><b>In short:</b> mind uploading is the idea of scanning a brain in enough detail to recreate the person's mind in a computer. Scientists have fully mapped the wiring of a tiny worm and a fruit fly, and a cubic millimetre of mouse brain, but a human brain has around 86 billion neurons. Uploading remains science fiction for now, though brain mapping itself is advancing quickly.</div>

<h2>What "uploading" would require</h2>
<p>The usual approach, called <b>whole brain emulation</b>, imagines three steps:</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li><b>Scan</b> a brain at very high resolution, capturing every neuron and connection (synapse).</li>
<li><b>Model</b> how each type of neuron and synapse behaves.</li>
<li><b>Simulate</b> the whole network on a computer fast enough to "run" it.</li>
</ol>
<p>The complete wiring diagram of a nervous system is called a <b>connectome</b>.</p>

<h2>How far brain mapping has come</h2>
<table><thead><tr><th>Organism</th><th>Neurons</th><th>Milestone</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>C. elegans worm</td><td>302</td><td>First complete connectome, published in 1986; the OpenWorm project still works to simulate it convincingly</td></tr>
<tr><td>Fruit fly (adult brain)</td><td>About 140,000</td><td>Full connectome published by the FlyWire consortium in 2024, with tens of millions of synapses</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mouse (one cubic millimetre)</td><td>Hundreds of thousands of cells in the sample</td><td>The MICrONS project mapped a cubic millimetre of visual cortex, published in 2025</td></tr>
<tr><td>Human</td><td>About 86 billion</td><td>Only tiny fragments mapped at synapse level so far</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Even with the worm's 302 neurons fully mapped for decades, simulating its behaviour accurately is still hard, which shows that wiring alone is not the whole story.</p>

<h2>The hard problems</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>Scale:</b> a human brain has on the order of 100 trillion synapses. Imaging one at that resolution would produce staggering amounts of data.</li>
<li><b>More than wiring:</b> chemical signals, gene activity, glial cells and the strength of each synapse all shape thought.</li>
<li><b>Computing power:</b> simulating every neuron in real time is far beyond today's largest supercomputers at the level of biological detail neuroscientists think may be needed.</li>
<li><b>Identity:</b> if a perfect copy ran on a computer, would it be <em>you</em>, or a new person who remembers being you? Philosophers have debated this for decades.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Where the research is actually useful today</h2>
<p>Connectomics helps explain how brains process vision and smell, how diseases disrupt circuits, and inspires new ideas in AI. Brain-computer interfaces, meanwhile, already help some people with paralysis control computers and robotic arms. These are real benefits, even if digital immortality is not on the horizon.</p>

<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Has anyone uploaded a mind?</h3>
<p>No. No animal mind, let alone a human one, has been uploaded and shown to behave like the original.</p>
<h3>Is AI the same as an uploaded mind?</h3>
<p>No. Large language models learn patterns from text; they are not copies of a particular brain.</p>
