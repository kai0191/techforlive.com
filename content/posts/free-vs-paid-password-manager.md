<h2>Free vs paid password manager: the short version</h2>
<p>A password manager is an app that remembers every login for you. You save each username and password once, in a locked vault. One master password opens the vault, and it is the only password you have to remember. After that, the manager fills in logins on your phone and computer, and it can create a long random password for every new account. That single change fixes the most common password habit: reusing one password everywhere.</p>
<p>Free and paid managers share the same core job. Both store logins, fill them in, and generate strong passwords. Both lock the vault with encryption, which scrambles your data so it cannot be read without your master password. The difference is the extras around that core. A built-in code generator, space for files, a way for family to get in if you cannot, and reports on weak or reused passwords. Paying buys convenience and backup options, not a stronger lock.</p>
<p>That is why the honest answer is conditional. If you only need your own logins saved and filled in, a free manager does the job. If you share logins with family, store scans of documents, or want codes and passwords in one app, a paid plan is worth a look. The rest of this guide shows what each side gives you, so you can decide in ten minutes and stop thinking about it.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Tip:</b> If you reuse one password everywhere, any password manager beats any plan choice. Start free today and upgrade later if you need to.</div>

<h2>What a free password manager gives you</h2>
<p>Bitwarden&#x27;s free plan is the clearest example. It stores unlimited passwords on unlimited devices, with no trial clock and no device limit. It also handles passkeys, which are a newer kind of login that lives on your device instead of a typed password. You can share a login with one other person for free, and you can send a secure note through its Send feature. The apps run on phones, computers, and browsers, and the vault syncs between them.</p>
<p>Your phone already has a free manager built in. Apple Passwords comes with an Apple Account on iPhones running iOS 18 or later, and it syncs through iCloud Keychain. It saves logins, passkeys, and Wi-Fi passwords, and it warns you about weak or reused ones. Google Password Manager does the same job for a Google account, inside Chrome and on Android phones. Both cost nothing extra, and both fill in logins automatically once you turn them on.</p>
<p>A free manager also covers the security that matters most. It creates a different long password for every site, so one breach cannot open your other accounts. It fills in the right login only on the real site, which helps against fake login pages. And it keeps your vault encrypted, so even the company running the service cannot read your passwords. None of that needs a subscription.</p>
<h2>What paying adds: four extras worth knowing</h2>
<p>Paid plans add features around the edges, and four of them matter. The first is a built-in authenticator, which generates the six-digit codes some sites ask for after your password. Bitwarden Premium includes it, so your codes and passwords live in one app. Without it, you need a separate authenticator app, which works fine but means one more app to keep. The second is file storage: Premium gives you encrypted space for scans and documents, while the free plan stores text only.</p>
<p>Third is emergency access. Bitwarden Premium lets you name a trusted person for emergency access. That person can ask for entry to your vault if you are locked out or unable to use it. You set a waiting period, and the request only goes through if you do not refuse it in time. The fourth is a health report, which lists weak, reused, or exposed passwords in your vault so you can fix the worst ones first. 1Password includes its own version, called Watchtower, on every paid plan. It also offers Travel Mode, which hides chosen vaults while you cross a border.</p>
<p>Family is the other paid difference. Bitwarden Families covers up to six people, each with a Premium account, and shared vaults for household logins. 1Password Families covers up to five people, with private vaults for each person and shared vaults for the household. If several people in one home share streaming, utility, and shopping logins, one family plan replaces a tangle of shared passwords.</p>

<h2>Who should pay, and who can stay free</h2>
<p>Stay free if the manager only serves you. A single person with one phone and one computer, using Bitwarden free or the built-in Apple or Google manager, loses nothing that matters. Your logins are stored, filled in, and protected by a unique password each. The paid extras would sit unused, and the cheapest paid plan is still a bill you have to remember. Try the free plan for a month first, and only pay if you hit a limit.</p>
<p>Pay if you share a household. Family plans make sense the moment two or more people share logins for streaming, bills, or a child&#x27;s school account. Sharing a password by text message or a note on the fridge is the habit a family plan replaces. A family plan also gives each person a private vault, so shared logins and private ones stay separate. For a household that shares a dozen accounts, one plan is simpler than a dozen shared passwords.</p>
<p>Pay if you want one app for everything. Some people prefer codes, files, and passwords in a single place, and they would rather pay a few dollars a month than juggle two apps. Bitwarden Premium costs a couple of dollars a month when billed yearly. 1Password Individual costs a few dollars a month billed yearly, with a lower first-year price for new customers. Both are cheap next to the cost of a hacked email account, but neither is needed for basic safety.</p>

<h2>How to switch to a password manager without locking yourself out</h2>
<p>Choose a master password you can remember but nobody can guess: four or five random words work well. Write it on paper and keep it somewhere safe at home, not in a note on your phone. If you forget it, there is no reset button: the vault is encrypted with it, and the company cannot open it for you. Some managers offer an emergency contact or a recovery key; set one up on day one.</p>
<p>The switch itself takes about half an hour, and most of that is waiting for the import. Do it on a quiet evening, not five minutes before a flight. Check that autofill works on your bank and email first, because those are the logins you will miss first if something went wrong. If a site does not fill in, check that the saved entry matches the site&#x27;s address before you change anything else.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Pick one manager and install it on your phone and your computer first. Sign in with the same account on both, so the vault syncs before you change anything.</li><li>Turn on autofill. On iPhone, go to Settings &gt; General &gt; AutoFill &amp; Passwords, turn on AutoFill Passwords and Passkeys, and pick your manager as the source. On Android, open Settings and search for autofill, then pick your manager as the autofill service.</li><li>Import your saved logins from your browser. Most managers offer an import step that reads the file your browser exports from its password settings. Delete that file as soon as the import finishes, because it holds your passwords in plain text.</li><li>Change your most important passwords first: email, banking, and the Apple or Google account itself. Let the manager create a new long password for each one, and save it before you move on. Your email matters most, because a hacker who controls it can reset your other accounts.</li><li>Keep the old manager or browser saving switched on for a week, in case a login did not import. Once autofill works everywhere and every login is in the new vault, turn the old one off. Only then cancel a paid plan you are leaving.</li></ol>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> Never email yourself the exported password file, and never store it in a cloud folder. It holds your passwords in plain text. Delete it right after the import.</div>
<h2>Myths you can ignore about password managers</h2>
<p>The first myth is that a free manager must be selling your data. Bitwarden is open source, which means anyone can read its code, and its free plan earns money by selling the paid plan, not your passwords. Your vault is encrypted before it leaves your device, so the company cannot read it either way. A free plan from a company with a clear paid product is a normal business model, not a trap.</p>
<p>The second myth is that the cloud makes a manager unsafe. Your vault is scrambled on your device before it is uploaded, and only your master password can unscramble it. A breach of the company&#x27;s servers would expose scrambled data, not your passwords. The real risk is a weak master password, or someone watching you type it. That is why the master password should be long and unique.</p>
<p>The third myth is that passkeys will replace password managers soon. A passkey replaces a typed password on sites that support it, and managers now store passkeys too. But most sites still ask for a password, and will for years. A manager that holds both passwords and passkeys is the practical choice, not a stopgap. The fourth myth is that one manager is the most secure and the rest are unsafe. Any well-known manager with strong encryption protects you; the best one is the one you will actually use every day.</p>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Is Bitwarden free really free forever?</h3>
<p>Yes. Its free plan has no time limit and covers unlimited passwords and devices. Bitwarden earns money from its paid plans, which add extras like a built-in authenticator and file storage.</p>
<h3>Does 1Password have a free plan?</h3>
<p>No. 1Password has no free plan; it offers a short free trial, then needs a paid subscription. If you want a free plan, Bitwarden or your phone&#x27;s built-in manager is the usual choice.</p>
<h3>Can I use a free manager on all my devices?</h3>
<p>Yes, with Bitwarden free and with Apple Passwords or Google Password Manager. All three sync across your phone and computer. The free plan is not limited to one device.</p>
<h3>What happens if I forget my master password?</h3>
<p>You are locked out. The vault is encrypted with that password, and the company cannot open it. Write the password on paper and store it safely, and set up emergency access or a recovery option if your manager offers one.</p>
<h3>Are passkeys better than passwords?</h3>
<p>A passkey is harder to steal than a password, because there is nothing to type and nothing to phish. But not every site supports passkeys yet. A password manager stores both, so use a passkey where it is offered and a strong password everywhere else.</p>
